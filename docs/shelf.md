# The shelf: what the books support

A library of 119 investing and trading books was read, summarised one by one, and cross-examined in groups
(the full work is public at https://github.com/ungaroni/the-whole-shelf). The principles were ingested into
`data/kb/library.json` (sources S-020 onward). This page is the short human version: what the evidence in that
library supports, where authors contradict each other, and what did not survive testing. It is research, not advice.

## Supported by evidence outside the author's own book
- Costs are the one lever that always works. Investors as a group earn the market return minus what they pay;
  index funds at a tenth of a percent beat the average active product after fees in nearly every long window (Bogle,
  Malkiel, S-017 for the Israeli version).
- Long-run equity return is roughly starting dividend yield plus earnings growth; changes in what people pay for a
  dollar of earnings net to almost nothing over a century (Bogle, Malkiel).
- A high cyclically adjusted P/E predicts weak returns over the following decade, not over the next quarter. It is a
  dial for expectations and saving, never a timing trigger (Shiller).
- Slow trend filters on broad indexes, such as a ten-month average or twelve-month momentum against bills, cut the
  worst drawdowns in simulation with about one trade a year. The same rule on single stocks trades far more often and is
  untested (Antonacci; Faith and Katz & McCormick on slow versus fast systems).
- Relative strength and momentum are the technical ideas with independent academic replication; most other objective
  chart rules were indistinguishable from luck once the number of rules tried was accounted for (Aronson, Kirkpatrick).
- Multi-week chart patterns carry a modest measured edge; single-week candlestick patterns mostly do not, and
  candle signals decay within about a week (Bulkowski, Morris).
- Exits and risk control decide survival more than entries: a better exit cut the loss on random entries by nearly half
  (Katz & McCormick, Eckhardt).
- Mistaking a lucky run for skill is the default error: among ten thousand coin-flipping managers, a few hundred show
  five winning years by chance (Taleb). Discount any record by the size of the cohort it came from.

## Where the authors disagree, and where the evidence points
- Index or pick stocks: index by default. Every picker in the library offers one self-reported record selected by
  outcome, and most of them tell the reader who will not do the work to index.
- Stops or hold and add: depends on leverage and thesis, not on taste. If a drawdown can force you out, use a hard stop.
  If you own it outright on a balance-sheet thesis, use a written falsification test instead. Do not mix the two.
- Buy strength or buy weakness: both camps have real records and no controlled evidence. Both demand a catalyst or a
  confirmation, and neither buys merely because something is cheap or merely because it is moving.
- Cheap or high quality: both together. Ranking on return on capital and earnings yield beat either alone (Greenblatt).
- Fixed-percent or volatility stops: volatility stops. A flat percentage is tight on a utility and meaningless on a
  volatile growth name (Tharp, Grimes).
- Psychology first or edge first: edge first, demonstrated with costs; discipline applied to a losing method only loses
  more consistently.

## Not supported
- Retail forex and binary options: zero-sum or negative-sum by construction, with loss rates the sellers themselves
  concede. Not investing.
- Harmonic patterns, Fibonacci grids, Elliott waves, Gann lines and financial astrology: no backtest anywhere in the
  library; unprogrammable and therefore untestable.
- Volume confirmation and divergence as triggers: asserted by many, failed the one author who tested them (Grimes).
- Named breadth and sentiment thresholds from before 2000: their authors say they degraded. Re-verify every number.
